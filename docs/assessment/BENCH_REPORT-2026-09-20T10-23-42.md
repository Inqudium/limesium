## 1. Executive summary

One read-state sample through the metrics owner costs **36-37 ns and 288 B** on today's HEAD and would cost **5.6 ns and 0 B** behind a cache in the shape legatium ended up with; the floor - `Counter.increment` on a counter already in hand - is **4.3 ns and 0 B**. The cache removes all of the step's allocation and 85 % of its time, and leaves the owner 1.3 ns above what the meter itself costs. The figures are the same with one tag set and with sixteen rotating ones - except for the one number that is not, and that one is the first run's mistake (section 4.1): a single constant template lets the JIT drop 56 of the 288 bytes, so the 2026-08-29 figure of "269 ± 21 B" was a hot-entry artefact, and 288 B is what a host pays.

Against the 2026-08-29 gates the result lands where it landed then: 31 ns against a 300 ns gate, 288 B against a 300 B gate. The gates were right for the question they were set for - "does the lookup cost grow with registry size, enough to matter?" - and that question stays answered: it does not. What they were not set for is the question legatium asked and this run repeats: what share of the step is avoidable, and what does avoiding it cost. The share is 85 % of the time and 100 % of the allocation; a measured exchange asks three times (request size, read state, response size), so about 95 ns and 860 B per measured exchange on the opt-in path. Relative to the emission that follows - a formatted line of a few hundred bytes plus its MDC scopes - that is a small share, as it was for legatium; it is nonetheless the largest avoidable allocation on that path.

The price side is what this report can say that the first could not. Legatium's cache is "a concurrent map and lifecycle coupling", as the first report warned, and the coupling bit: a registration inside `computeIfAbsent` against a removal listener under the registry lock, a deadlock of the host's whole registry, one chance in sixteen per coincidence of a first-time tag set with a removal. The fix is known and small - resolve the meter outside the map, publish with `putIfAbsent` - and leaves a documented residual window in which a meter removed during a first registration is cached detached until the owner is recreated. Variant B here is measured in exactly that fixed shape, listener included, and costs nothing on the hit path for it.

**Verdict - accepted, reversing the 2026-08-29 refutation.** The refutation answered the scaling hypothesis, which remains dead; on the question of the step's own cost the numbers are the same class as legatium's (there 416 B and 49 ns per sample avoidable, here 288 B and 31 ns; the difference is legatium's third tag), and the cost of the cache is now a known quantity - a map, a key, a listener, a lock-order rule with a residue, and the two tests legatium wrote for the two ways a cache can be wrong - rather than the open "lifecycle coupling" the first report priced at infinity. The owner on this HEAD is unchanged; the port is a follow-up change (section 6), and the benchmark stays as its acceptance check. A host will not notice either way (section 5.3); the reason to do it is the same as for the other confirmed cleanups of the 2026-08-29 series: a measured, bounded, zero-risk-when-done-right reduction on a path whose purpose is to be cheap enough to leave on.

## 2. The two variants, and the floor

Everything measured lives in `eu.inqudium.limesium.common.EndpointLoggingMetrics`, the metrics owner both endpoint-logging twins share (ADR-0003 amendment of 2026-09-05; ONE instance per registry through `forRegistry`, so a host carrying both twins on one registry has one owner). The method under measurement is `requestBodyRead`, the read-state counter; `requestBodySize` and `responseBodySize` go through the private `recordBodySize`, the same shape on a `DistributionSummary` with one tag fewer. The benchmark measures the counter path because it is the one with two tags and therefore the more expensive of the three; the summary path differs only in the meter type and has one `Tags` merge less.

### 2.1 Variant A - register per call (the owner on this HEAD)

```kotlin
fun requestBodyRead(
    template: String?,
    state: BodyReadState,
) = registerOrFallback(REQUEST_BODY_READ_METER) { registry ->
    Counter
        .builder(REQUEST_BODY_READ_METER)
        .description("Exchanges by how far the application consumed the request body: unread, partial, or complete")
        .tag("uri", template ?: UNTEMPLATED_URI)
        .tag("state", state.tagValue)
        .register(registry)
}.increment()
```

Every call runs the whole chain: a `Counter.Builder` is created, two `.tag()` calls each merge one more tag into a fresh sorted `Tags` instance (Micrometer's `Tags` is immutable; `tags.and(key, value)` copies and sorts), `register` builds a `Meter.Id` from name, tags and description and hands it to the registry. The registry (section 2.3) finds the counter it already holds and returns it; `increment` then adds one. The `registerOrFallback` wrapper adds a try/catch around `register` and nothing on the happy path.

Nothing in this chain is wrong. Micrometer's own documentation shows exactly this shape for meters with dynamic tags, and the registry guarantees that the second call returns the meter the first one created. The question was only what the chain costs when it runs three times per measured exchange for the lifetime of a host - and the 2026-08-29 answer, "37 ns and up to 288 B, flat over registry size", is reproduced here to the byte (section 3).

The benchmark measures this variant as `registerPerCall`: the production method itself, `metrics.requestBodyRead(template, COMPLETE)`, on the real (internal, bytecode-public) owner obtained through `forRegistry` exactly as a twin obtains it.

### 2.2 Variant B - resolve once per tag set, cache the meter (legatium's fixed shape, benchmark-local)

The owner on this HEAD has no cache; variant B is the shape legatium's `ClientLoggingMetrics` has after its finding 1 was fixed, built inside the benchmark against the same registry and the same `Meter.Id`:

```java
/** The folded tag values of one read-state counter - two references, 24 B with compressed oops. */
private record BodyMeterKey(String uriTemplate, String state) {}

private final ConcurrentHashMap<BodyMeterKey, Counter> readStateCounters = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();

// in setup, once per owner:
registry.config().onMeterRemoved(removed -> readStateCounters.values().removeIf(it -> it == removed));

private Counter cachedCounter(String template) {
    BodyMeterKey key = new BodyMeterKey(template == null ? UNTEMPLATED_URI : template, STATE.getTagValue());
    Counter hit = readStateCounters.get(key);
    if (hit != null) {
        return hit;
    }
    Counter fresh = counterFor(key.uriTemplate());       // the builder chain of variant A, OUTSIDE the map
    Counter raced = readStateCounters.putIfAbsent(key, fresh);
    return raced != null ? raced : fresh;
}
```

The builder chain is the same as in variant A - it now runs once per tag set, on the first sample for that template and state. Every later sample builds the key, looks it up, and increments. Four things about the shape are deliberate, three of them legatium's and one the lesson of its finding 1:

- **The key is built from the folded tag values, not the raw inputs.** A null template folds to `UNKNOWN` before the key exists, the same folding the tag gets. Two raw inputs that yield the same meter id therefore share one entry, and the cache holds exactly one entry per meter the registry holds under that name. That is why it needs no size policy of its own: its cardinality *is* the registry's, bounded by the same rule - the `uri` tag is the framework's best-matching handler pattern (`HandlerMapping.BEST_MATCHING_PATTERN_ATTRIBUTE`, read by both twins), a value from the application's route table, never from traffic. Section 5 derives the expected number of entries.
- **The plain `get` comes before any write.** On the hit path - every sample but the first per tag set - the `get` finds the entry and no lambda, no builder and no `Tags` are ever built. Legatium's first run measured 72 B/op with a bare `computeIfAbsent`, whose capturing lambda is allocated on every call, hit or miss; the `get` first took that off the hit path.
- **The miss path resolves OUTSIDE the map and publishes with `putIfAbsent`, never `computeIfAbsent`.** This is legatium's finding 1. `ConcurrentHashMap.computeIfAbsent` runs its mapping function under the bin's monitor; `Counter.Builder.register` on a new id enters Micrometer's `synchronized (meterMapLock)`; and `MeterRegistry.remove` notifies `onMeterRemoved` listeners *inside* that same lock, where the listener's `removeIf` takes the bin lock. Two threads, two locks, opposite order: the registering exchange and the removing host wait on each other forever, and every later registration on the registry from any library queues behind them. With the resolution outside the map there is no bin lock held during registration; a lost race registers the same id twice, which Micrometer deduplicates to one instance anyway, and `putIfAbsent` keeps whichever landed first. The residue legatium documents and accepts: between the registration returning and the `putIfAbsent` lies a window of microseconds in which a host removal of that very meter runs the listener against a cache that holds no entry yet; the detached instance is then published and takes every later sample of its tag set until the owner is recreated. It needs a host removing meters at runtime AND the removal in that window, and costs the samples of one tag set - never an event.
- **The removal listener closes the one way cache and registry can drift apart.** `MeterRegistry.remove` is the only operation that makes a cached reference stale; a host that calls it (rare, but part of the API - Kafka client metrics on a rebalance, a registry `clear()`, a pruner) would otherwise have the owner recording into a meter no exporter reads. The listener drops the entry, and the next sample registers anew. It runs under the registry lock and takes bin locks, which is fine exactly because nothing else ever holds a bin lock while wanting the registry lock.

The benchmark measures this variant as `cached`: `cachedCounter(template).increment()`, on a cache warmed for every tag set in setup - the steady state a long-running host sees.

### 2.3 What Micrometer does on `register` - why "the registry deduplicates" was right and yet costs

`Counter.Builder.register(registry)` (Micrometer 1.17.1) is:

```java
public Counter register(MeterRegistry registry) {
    return register(registry, tags);
}

private Counter register(MeterRegistry registry, Tags tags) {
    return registry.counter(new Meter.Id(name, tags, baseUnit, description, Type.COUNTER));
}
```

and `MeterRegistry.counter(Meter.Id)` goes through `registerMeterIfNecessary` into `getOrCreateMeter`, whose first lines are:

```java
Meter m = preFilterIdToMeterMap.get(originalId);
if (m != null && !isStaleId(originalId)) {
    checkAndWarnAboutDoubleRegistration(m);
    return m;
}
Meter.Id mappedId = mapId(originalId);
m = meterMap.get(mappedId);
...
```

So on a repeat registration the registry does one `ConcurrentHashMap` lookup keyed by the *original* id, before any `MeterFilter` runs, and returns the existing meter. That is the deduplication the 2026-08-29 report relied on, and it is cheap and O(1) - the flat curve from 10 to 5 000 meters was that lookup. What it cannot avoid is everything that happens *before* the lookup in the caller - the builder, the two `Tags` merges and the `Meter.Id` that `register` builds so it has something to look up with. The 288 B of variant A are those objects; the 0 B of variant B are what remains when the owner's own lookup needs only a key the JIT can see through (section 4.1).

(The same subtlety as in legatium's report applies: older Micrometer versions ran the filter chain's `map` on every registration before the lookup; since the pre-filter map exists, they do not. On such a version the time gap would be larger. The allocation gap is the same either way.)

### 2.4 The floor - `increment` on a resolved counter

```java
resolved[slot()].increment();
```

`Counter.increment` on a `SimpleMeterRegistry` counter: a `CumulativeCounter` adds to a `DoubleAdder`. No allocation, about 4.3 ns on this machine. Nothing above it in either variant can go below this; the distance to it is what the variant costs. (Legatium's floor is 28 ns because its measured meter is a `DistributionSummary`, whose `record` updates a count, a total and a windowed max; the counter's floor is the cheaper of the two, so the ratios here are, if anything, harder on the cache than legatium's.)

### 2.5 What the benchmark does, and the two traps it avoids

`BodyMeterBenchmark` is one `@State(Scope.Benchmark)` class with a `SimpleMeterRegistry`, the owner obtained through `EndpointLoggingMetrics.forRegistry` exactly as a twin obtains it, and `tagSets` ∈ {1, 16} URI templates (`/api/things/<i>/{id}`). Each operation increments the read-state counter for `COMPLETE` under the next template in rotation (a mask, since the counts are powers of two). Three benchmark methods: `registerPerCall` (variant A, the production method), `cached` (variant B) and `incrementOnly` (the floor, on counters resolved once in setup).

- **Rotation over tag sets.** With one tag set a benchmark measures one hot entry that stays in a register or an L1 line - and, this run shows, one constant template that the JIT partially sees through: variant A allocates 232 B with one template and 288 B with sixteen, in every fork (section 4.1). The 2026-08-29 run had one constant template and no rotation; its "269 ± 21 B" at `registrySize = 10` is three forks disagreeing about how much of the chain to scalar-replace, and its 288 B at 500 and 5 000 is the number without that help. Sixteen rotating entries make both the map and the builder chain do real work. That the `cached` and `incrementOnly` columns agree within 0.1 ns between one and sixteen says the map read is not the cost.
- **Warmed cache.** Setup increments once per template through the owner and once through the cache, so no measurement iteration sees a miss. The miss path is variant A plus a map insert, once per tag set per process lifetime; it is not what a host pays per exchange and it is not measured.
- **No blackhole games.** `increment` mutates registry state, which is a side effect the JIT cannot eliminate, so the methods return `void`. The object escapes into the registry by construction.
- **The retired axis.** The first run's `registrySize` parameter (10 / 500 / 5 000 unrelated meters in the host registry) answered its question - flat - and is not swept again; the registry here holds the benchmark's own meters only. A reader who wants the scaling evidence has it in `body-meter.txt`.

## 4. Findings

### 4.1 Allocation: 288 B down to 0 B per sample - Confirmed, with a caveat about what "0" is

Variant A allocates 288 B per sample under rotating templates: a `Counter.Builder` (its `Tags` field starts empty), two `Tags` copies from the two `.tag()` calls (each a new sorted array of `Tag` objects plus the `ImmutableTag` for the new pair), and the `Meter.Id`. With one constant template it allocates 232 B - 56 B less, one `Tags` step's worth - because C2, seeing the same string every call, scalar-replaces part of the chain. That is not a number a host sees; a host has many templates. The 2026-08-29 run's "269 ± 21 B" was this effect averaged over three forks, and 288 B is the figure this report carries.

Variant B allocates nothing measurable (`≈ 10⁻⁴ B/op`, the profiler's floor). The key - a Java `record` with two reference fields, 24 B with compressed oops - is built on every call in the source, and the JIT removes it: `ConcurrentHashMap.get` inlines, the record's `hashCode` and `equals` inline, the key never escapes, escape analysis scalar-replaces it. This is the same key legatium measured at 32 B on the Kotlin data class it built - which the JIT there did *not* remove, presumably because the Kotlin `?:` chain with the fallback lambda in the same method kept the key alive across a call the compiler would not inline. So the honest expectation for a Kotlin port of variant B into the owner is 0-32 B per sample, decided by the JIT's view of the method it lands in, and the benchmark of the port must re-measure it rather than assume the 0 of this run. Either end of that range is a rounding error against 288.

Per measured exchange (three samples: request size, read state, response size; the summaries have one tag fewer and cost correspondingly a little less): about 860 B before, 0-96 B after. At 1 000 measured exchanges per second that is up to 0.86 MB/s of short-lived garbage that no longer exists - a young-generation load, never a leak, but a load that scaled with the traffic on a path whose purpose is to be cheap enough to leave on.

### 4.2 Time: 37 ns down to 5.6 ns per sample, 1.3 ns above the floor - Confirmed

Variant A takes 36-37 ns, variant B 5.6 ns, the floor 4.3 ns. The 1.3 ns between B and the floor are the key's hash (two `String.hashCode` reads, cached in the strings, combined by the record) and the map read. The 31 ns between A and B are the builder chain and the id.

The absolute figures are JDK 26 on a turbo-enabled workstation; the ratio - variant B at 15 % of variant A's time - is the transferable number. On a target JDK 21 the builder chain would not be cheaper (it is allocation-bound), so the ratio is, if anything, conservative. The 2026-08-29 run with turbo disabled put variant A at 37-39 ns; the clock made no visible difference to a path this short.

### 4.3 Independent of the number of tag sets - Confirmed

One versus sixteen rotating tag sets changes variant B by 0.07 ns and the floor by nothing; variant A by 0.9 ns and by the 56 B of section 4.1. The cache's cost is not that of a hot single entry, and Micrometer's lookup is not helped by one either. A host with a few hundred handler patterns would sit on the same numbers until the map exceeds a cache level, which at 24-byte keys and a few hundred entries it does not.

### 4.4 What the 2026-08-29 decision got right, and what it did not ask - Assessed

The recorded decision was: both gates missed (35 ns ≪ 300 ns; 269-288 B < 300 B), the scaling concern disproven (hash-based O(1) lookup, flat from 10 to 5 000 meters), and "the proposed meter cache would add a concurrent map and lifecycle coupling to save ~35 ns/request". Every one of those statements is reproduced here and stands. The two gates were set for the finding as the input analysis worded it - a per-emission cost that "additionally grows with total meter count" - and against that hypothesis 300 ns and 300 B were the right bar: nothing that stays flat and under a third of a microsecond deserves a cache for scaling's sake.

What the decision did not ask - and could not have answered without the numbers legatium produced three weeks later - is the share. Of the step's 37 ns, 31 are avoidable; of its 288 B, all are. The gates measured the step against an absolute bar; the share measures it against itself, and against the floor no design can go below. On that reading the step is the same class as legatium's: there 84 → 35 ns and 448 → 32 B, here 37 → 5.6 ns and 288 → 0 B; the difference between the two owners is one tag (legatium's `host`, `name` and `uri` against limesium's `uri` and `state`), one `Tags` merge, and a summary instead of a counter at the floor.

And what the decision priced at infinity - "lifecycle coupling" - is now a known quantity. Legatium built it, the next defect analysis found the deadlock in it, the fix is four lines and a KDoc rule, the residue is documented, and two tests pin the two ways a cache of meter references can be wrong against a live registry. The price did not go to zero; it went from unknown to known, and known is what a decision needs.

### 4.5 The two correctness cases are legatium's tests, not this measurement - Recorded

A cache of meter references has exactly two ways to be wrong against a live registry, and neither shows in a throughput number. Legatium's `ClientLoggingMetricsTest` pins both; a port to limesium brings both tests with it, and they are the acceptance criterion the benchmark cannot be:

- **A host removes a meter.** `MeterRegistry.remove` makes the cached reference stale; the owner would record into an instance no exporter reads. The test removes a summary and a counter after a sample and asserts that the next sample lands in a *new* meter the registry holds (count 1, the new amount) while the removed instance saw nothing more.
- **A host rejects a meter's id.** Micrometer refuses a registration whose id exists with another type; the owner's `registerOrFallback` keeps such a meter in its private registry with one warning. Behind the cache this path runs once per tag set, and the test pins that three samples against a host gauge under the counter's exact id neither throw nor reach the host, and warn once.

A third case is not a test but a rule: the lock order of section 2.2. It cannot be pinned deterministically (the deadlock needs a first-time tag set and a removal to share a `ConcurrentHashMap` bin at the same instant), so legatium fixes it in the class KDoc and in the shape of the miss path - resolve outside, `putIfAbsent` - and this report records that the benchmark's variant B was measured in exactly that shape, so the numbers above are the numbers of the safe version.

## 5. Expected cardinality of the cache

The cache has no bound of its own, so the question a reviewer must be able to answer is how many entries it holds in a real host and why that number cannot run away on its own. The answer follows from the key, and in limesium it is shorter than in legatium.

### 5.1 What the key is made of

Every entry is one `BodyMeterKey(uriTemplate, state)` for the counter map and one `(meterName, uriTemplate)` for the summary map (a port would use one key type with a nullable `state`, as legatium does), built from the FOLDED tag values - the same values the meter's `Meter.Id` carries:

| Component | Source | Values | Bounded by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `meterName` | fixed | 2 in the summary map (`endpoint.request.body.size`, `endpoint.response.body.size`), 1 in the counter map | the code |
| `uriTemplate` (the `uri` tag) | the framework's best-matching handler pattern for the request (`HandlerMapping.BEST_MATCHING_PATTERN_ATTRIBUTE`; the servlet twin reads the request attribute after the chain, the reactive twin the exchange attribute at the terminal signal); `UNKNOWN` when no handler matched (a 404, a static resource, an error dispatch) | R + 1, where R is the number of distinct handler patterns the application maps | the application's route table - a pattern is an annotation or a router-function literal in the application's code, not a value from traffic; a path that matches no pattern folds to `UNKNOWN` |
| `state` | `BodyReadState`, counter map only | 3 (`unread`, `partial`, `complete`) | the code |

The components are the meter's tags. That is the whole argument in one sentence: the cache cannot hold an entry the registry does not hold a meter for, because both are keyed on the same folded values, and it cannot hold two entries for one meter, because the folding happens before the key is built. Limesium has no host and no client-name dimension - the endpoint side's only dynamic tag is the route, and the route is the framework's, already low-cardinality by the same argument `http.server.requests` rests on.

### 5.2 The bound, and what a host actually sees

Per owner - one per registry, shared by both twins:

- summary map: at most 2 · (R + 1) entries,
- counter map: at most 3 · (R + 1) entries,
- together at most **5 · (R + 1)**.

The bound is loose in practice, in the registry's favour and the cache's alike:

- A size summary exists only for a route that saw a body of more than zero bytes in that direction (the zero-byte rule of `recordBodySize`). The request-side summary of a GET route never exists; the response-side summary of a `204` route neither.
- A read-state counter exists only for a state that occurred: a route whose body is always read to the end has one counter, not three; a route the framework parses itself (form and multipart) has only `unread`.
- The summaries exist only with `measure-request-body-size` resp. `measure-response-body-size` on; the counter with the former. All are opt-in and off by default - a host that does not measure has an empty cache.

Worked examples, all per owner. The "typical" column applies the three rules above with one assumption each: half the routes take a body (request summary on half the routes), nearly all answer with one (response summary on most), and a route shows one or two read states - together about 3 of the 5 possible entries per route, so 60 % of the bound. The last two columns price the bound at 60 B per cache entry (a `ConcurrentHashMap` node of 32 B plus a 24-byte key plus its share of the table; the strings are the meter's own) and at 300 B per meter, the low end of what a `SimpleMeterRegistry` summary holds (a Prometheus or OTLP histogram is kilobytes):

| Scenario | R | Bound | Typical (60 %) | Cache at bound | Registry at bound |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A small service, eight routes | 8 | 45 | 27 | 2.7 KB | ≥ 14 KB |
| A typical application, forty routes | 40 | 205 | 123 | 12 KB | ≥ 62 KB |
| A large monolith, three hundred routes | 300 | 1 505 | 903 | 90 KB | ≥ 450 KB |
| Pathological: a host records the raw path as the template (a custom `MeterFilter` or a wrapper rewriting the attribute), ten thousand distinct paths seen | 10 000 | 50 005 | 30 003 | 3 MB | ≥ 15 MB |

Two things the table shows. First, in every row but the last, R is a count of things in the application's code - route annotations, router-function entries - so the number is fixed at deployment and does not move with request rate, payload, tenants or users; a service sits in the tens to low hundreds of entries, a monolith around a thousand, at kilobytes to a hundred kilobytes for the cache. Second, the last row is not something the twins can produce - they read the framework's pattern, and Spring folds the path to the pattern before the attribute exists - and it is a registry problem before it is a cache problem: the 3 MB of cache sit next to at least 15 MB of meters, and `http.server.requests` on the same registry explodes first and by more. The ratio of the two last columns - one to five in every row - is the point: the cache never costs more than a fraction of what the host has already accepted for the meters themselves.

### 5.3 What the result buys a host, in the host's units

At the 2026-08-29 report's operating point - 500 measured req/s - variant A's three samples cost about 0.43 MB/s and 0.005 % of one core; variant B's about nothing. At 5 000 measured req/s the numbers are ten times that: 4.3 MB/s, 0.05 % of a core. No host will see either figure on a dashboard, and this report does not claim otherwise; the emission's own log line and MDC scopes cost an order of magnitude more per exchange than the three samples ever did. The claim is narrower: the step is the largest avoidable allocation on the opt-in path, its avoidance is measured, bounded and now safely shaped, and the owner is the one place in both twins where a per-exchange allocation of this size exists for no reason but the shape of a builder API.

### 5.4 Why the cache deliberately has no bound of its own

Three reasons, in the order they matter - the same three as legatium's, and they hold with fewer dimensions:

1. **A bound would not limit anything.** The cardinality is the registry's, decided by the tags; the cache mirrors it. An eviction policy on the cache would evict entries whose meters stay in the registry and re-pay variant A for them - 288 B and 31 ns per sample on the evicted routes - while the registry keeps every meter.
2. **A bound would say the wrong thing.** A `maxEntries` on the cache reads as "the module limits body-meter cardinality", which only the framework's pattern folding does. A second knob on the cache would invite tuning the symptom.
3. **The monotone growth is the registry's own.** Micrometer registries do not evict meters either; an entry that exists once exists for the process lifetime, or until `MeterRegistry.remove`, which the listener honours. The cache and the registry grow in lockstep and shrink in lockstep; there is no state the cache can accumulate that the registry does not.

## 6. What the result says, and what it does not

- It says the per-sample resolution is the largest avoidable allocation on the opt-in body-measurement path, that all of it and 85 % of the step's time can go, and that the shape which removes it is known, debugged and priced - the 2026-08-29 verdict is reversed on that evidence, not on new numbers.
- It says the 2026-08-29 report was right about everything it measured: the numbers reproduce, the scaling hypothesis is dead, and "lifecycle coupling" was exactly the thing that later bit legatium. The first report's error, if it is one, was to stop at the gates instead of asking for the share; its warning was the more valuable half.
- It says nothing about the emission as a whole. A measured exchange's emission formats a log line of several hundred bytes, opens MDC scopes, renders up to two body captures and records the three samples; the samples' share of that was small before and is negligible after. A host that turns body measurement on pays for it in the meters' own state, not in the owner's bookkeeping.
- It says nothing about hosts whose meter update is expensive (a Prometheus histogram with many buckets, an OTLP exponential histogram). Both variants call the same `increment`/`record`; the change moves nothing there.
- It does not measure the miss path (first sample per route per process): variant A plus one map insert, once. Nor the lock order: that is a rule and a shape, pinned by KDoc and by the port's tests, not by a number.
- It does not implement the port. The owner on this HEAD is variant A. The follow-up is legatium's `ClientLoggingMetrics` cache brought into `EndpointLoggingMetrics` with the two-component key of section 5.1, the `get`-then-resolve-outside-then-`putIfAbsent` miss path, the removal listener, the lock-order KDoc with its residue, and the two tests of section 4.5; the acceptance check is this benchmark's `registerPerCall` cell moving onto the `cached` cell, 0-32 B and within a few nanoseconds of the floor.

## 7. Side observations

- The scalar replacement of the key (section 4.1) is a general point worth keeping for future benchmarks in this module: a `record` or a Kotlin data class used only as a map key may cost 0 or its full size depending on whether the JIT inlines the whole lookup into the caller. `gc.alloc.rate.norm` is "bytecode-determined" only for allocations the JIT cannot prove dead; for small keys and small methods it is JIT-determined, and a port must be re-measured in its own method, not assumed from a benchmark's.
- The 232 B against 288 B for one constant template against sixteen rotating (section 4.1) is the same lesson from the other side: a benchmark with one constant input measures the JIT's opinion of that input. The 2026-08-29 run's `registrySize` sweep was rotating the *haystack* and holding the *needle* constant; today's sweep rotates the needle. Both were needed; the second is the one that gives the host's number.
- JMH 1.37 on JDK 26 prints the `sun.misc.Unsafe::objectFieldOffset` deprecation warning at every fork start (from `org.openjdk.jmh.util.Utils`); it is JMH's, not the code under test's, and it does not affect the measurement. The `.mvn/jvm.config` flags the reactor uses for the same warning class do not apply to a plain `java -jar` run.
